\subsection{Lie Derivatives}

Consider a manifold $M$.

Define a {\bf path} to be a map

\beq
\gamma(\cdot:\pi):[0, 1]\rarrow M,
\quad \tau\mapsto \gamma(\tau;\pi)
\eeq
where $\pi\in M$  is a parameter of the function $\gamma(\cdot;\pi)$.
For example, $\tau$ might be time and 
$\pi$ might be the initial value
of $\gamma(\cdot;\pi)$, i.e., $\pi=\gamma(0;\pi)$.

Define a {\bf flow}  to be a map

\beq
\phi(\cdot;\tau):M \rarrow M,
\quad \pi\mapsto 
\phi(\pi;\tau)=\gamma(\tau;\pi)
\eeq
where $\tau\in[0,1]$  is a parameter of the function $\phi(\cdot;\tau)$.
Thus, $\phi(\cdot;\tau)$ moves points
on the manifold $M$ and is parametrized by a \qt{time} $\tau\in[0,1]$.

Note that we can think of $\phi(\cdot;\tau)$ as being an $M$-slice (at fixed $\tau$)
and of $\gamma(\cdot;\pi)$
as being a $\tau$-slice (at fixed $\pi$)
of a surface
$\Phi:M\times [0,1]\rarrow M$
such that 

\beq
\Phi(\tau, \pi)=\gamma(\tau;\pi)=\phi(\tau;\pi)
\eeq

The Lie derivative $\call_\vecX$
can be defined in terms of pullbacks
a follows. Consider the action of 
$\call_\vecX$ on the metric function
$\metric(\vecU, \vecV)$ as an example.



\beq
\call_{\vecX_\pi}
\metric_\pi(\vecU_\pi ,\vecV_\pi)
=
\left(\AT{\frac{d}{d\tau}}{\tau=0}
\pback{\pi}{\phi(\pi;\tau)}
{\metric_\pi}
\right)(\vecU_\pi ,\vecV_\pi)
=
\left[\AT{\frac{d}{d\tau}}{\tau=0}
\metric_{\gamma(\tau;\pi)}\right]
(\vecU_{\pi},
 \vecV_{\pi})
\eeq
where 
\beq
\phi(\pi;\tau=0)=\pi
\eeq

\beq
\vecX_{\gamma(\tau;\pi)}=\dot{\vgamma}(\tau;\pi)
\eeq

A vector field $\vecX$ is said to be a {\bf Killing field of the metric $\metric$} if

\beq
\call_\vecX \metric=0
\eeq


The Lie derivative
measure how a quantity
changes along a trajectory.
It's defined for vector fields
and dif-forms

The {\bf Lie Derivative $\call_\vecV$  of a vector field
$\vec{U}$} equals

\beq
\call_\vecV\vecU= [\vecU, \vecV]\in\gerX(M)
\eeq

\beq
\begin{array}{cc}
\bcen
\xymatrix{
& \ar[dl]|{\oper{\vecU}}
\\
\call_\vecV \vecU(\vecr)
&&\vecr
\ar[ul]|{\oper{\vecV}}
\ar[dl]|{\oper{\vecU}}
\\
&\ar[ul]|{-\oper{\vecV}}
}\ecen
&
\call_{\vecV}\vecU(\vecr)=
\left[
\oper{\vecV}, \oper{\vecU}
\right](\vecr)\in \RR
\end{array}
\eeq

Coordinate expression

The {\bf Lie derivative of a dif-form}
is given by
{\bf Cartan's Magic Formula}:

\beq
\begin{array}{cc}
\bcen
\xymatrix{
&\ar[dl]|{d}
\\
\call_\vecX \pform{\omega}{p}
&& \pform{\omega}{p}
\ar[ul]|{i_\vecX}
\ar[dl]|d
\\
&\ar[ul]|{i_\vecX}
}\ecen
&
\boxed
{\call_\vecX = i_\vecX d + d\;i_\vecX}
\end{array}
\eeq
In Cartan's Magic Formula
\begin{itemize}
\item exterior derivative $d(\;)$ gives change at a point.

\item {Lie derivative} $\call_\vecX(\;)$ gives
change along a trajectory
\end{itemize}




$d(\;)$ raises the degree of a dif-form by 1 and $i_\vecX(\;)$
lowers it by one,
so both summands in Cartan Magic Formula
leave the degree
of a dif-form
unchanged.

\begin{itemize}
\item 0-forms:

\beqa
\call_{\vecX}f(\cdot)
&=&
i_\vecX(df) + \cancel{d(i_\vecX f)}^0
\\
&=&
\ul{df}(\vecX)
\\
&=&
\vecX\cdot \nabla f
=  \vecX_{op}(f)
\eeqa
directional derivative

\item 1-form:

Suppose if
$\omega$
and $\vecX$ 
are defined
as follows:

\beq
\ul{\omega}=
\sum_{i=1}^n
\omega_i(x)\ul{dx^i}
\eeq
and

\beq
\vecX =\sum_i X^i(x) \vecpder{x^i}
\eeq

\beq
i_\vecX\ul{\omega}=
\ul{\omega}(\vecX)=
\omega_i X^i
\eeq

\beq
\boxed
{d(i_\vecX \ul{\omega})=
\partial_j
(\omega_i X^i)
\ul{dx^j}}
\label{eq-cmf-part1}
\eeq

\beqa
d\ul{\omega}
&=&
d \left[ \omega_i
\ul {dx^i}\right]
\\
&=&
\partial_j{\omega_i}
\ul{dx^j}\wedge
\ul{dx^i}
\\
&=&
\frac{1}{2}
[\partial_j \omega_i -\partial_i\omega_j]
\ul{dx^j}\wedge
\ul{dx^i}
\eeqa

\beq
i_\vecX
[\ul{dx^j}
\wedge \ul{dx^i}]=
X^j \ul{dx^i}-
X^i \ul{dx^j}
\eeq

\beq
\boxed
{i_\vecX
d\ul{\omega}=
\partial_j\omega_i
\left[
X^j \ul{dx^i}-
X^i\ul{dx^j}
\right]}
\label{eq-cmf-part2}
\eeq

Combining Eq.(\ref{eq-cmf-part1}) and Eq.(\ref{eq-cmf-part2}), we finally get

\beqa
\call_\vecX \pform{\omega}{}
&=&
\left(
X^j\partial_j
\omega_k
+\omega_j \partial_k X^j
\right)
\pform{dx^k}{}
\\
&=&
\vecX\cdot\nabla \pform{\omega}{}
-
\omega_j
\pform{d X^j}{}
\eeqa

\end{itemize}

\subsection{Integral Curves}

Vector fields generate integral curves.
Here is the definition of integral curves and related terminology.


We will call a function  $\vgamma: [0, 1]\rarrow M$
a {\bf path or curve} on
a manifold $M$. The domain 
is irrelevant for doing non-rigorous calculus as we are doing,
as long as it's some closed 
interval of real numbers.

Note 
that we will be abusing  notation
with regard to vector signs. 
To be precise

\beq
\xymatrix@C=5pc{
\gamma(t)\in M
\ar[r]_{
\begin{tabular}{c}
local 
\\ chart
\\ map
\end{tabular}
}
&
\vgamma(t)\in \RR^n
\ar[r]_{\begin{tabular}{c}
$\frac{d}{dt}$
\end{tabular}}
& [\dot{\gamma^i}(t)]\in \RR^n
\ar[r]_{
\begin{tabular}{c}
uncharting 
\\
map
\end{tabular}
}
& \dot{\vgamma}(t)\in T(M)
}
\eeq
We will be using
$\vgamma(t)$ for both 
$\gamma(t)$ and $\vgamma(t)$ as 
there is a bijection between them. We will also be using
$\dot{\vgamma}(t)$ for
both $[\dot{\gamma}^i(t)]$
and $\dot{\vgamma}(t)$
as there is a bijection between them too.
As physicists usually do, we define
a vector as any array with $n$
components, and put a vector
sign over all such arrays.
We define
 a {\bf position vector} as a vector that 
measures position relative to an origin,
and a {\bf displacement vector}
as a vector that measures
the separation between 2 positions and is thus origin independent.
The set of position vectors in $\RR^n$ is not even a vector space 
in the mathematicians' sense because
the difference of two position vectors is no
longer a position vector.
That's why affine spaces
were invented.

It's important to realize that neither $M$ nor
$T(M)$ 
\begin{enumerate}
\item have components
\item have an origin.
All its vectors are
displacement vectors
\end{enumerate} 
One must use charts to get 1 and 2.


We say {\bf curve $\vgamma$ is tangent to a vector field $\vecX$} if

\beq
\dot{\vgamma}(\tau)=
\vecX_{\vgamma(\tau)}
\label{eq-gamma-para-X}
\eeq
for all  $\tau\in[0,1]$.
In such a case we will write
\beq
\vecX_{\vgamma(\tau)} = \vecX^{\para \vgamma}_{\vgamma(\tau)}
\eeq

A curve $\vgamma$ that
satisfies Eq.(\ref{eq-gamma-para-X})
is called an {\bf integral curve}
of the vector field $\vecX$.
Note that curves, and in particular 
integral curves,
are 1 dimensional objects.

A {\bf congruence} is a family of integral curves that fills some region of the manifold, with exactly one curve passing through each point of the region.
Normally, one 
specifies a 
congruence as a 
set of curves
$\{\vgamma(\cdot: s)\mid s\in [a, b]\}$
where $s$ is a parameter and

\beq
\partial_\tau 
\vgamma(\tau; s)=
\vecX_{\vgamma(\tau; s)}
\eeq

A {\bf streamline}
is an integral 
curve of a velocity  field in Fluid
Mechanics.
If a fluid has a velocity field
$\vecV(\vecx, t)$, where
$\vecx$ is a 
position vector and
$t$ is time,
then a streamline
$\vgamma$ satisfies

\beq
\partial_\tau 
\vgamma(\tau)=
\vecV(\vgamma(\tau), t)
\eeq
Note that a streamline is not generally the {\bf particle trajectory} of an individual fluid particle.
For that, one solves

\beq
\AT{\partial_\tau 
\vgamma(\tau)}{\tau=t}
=
\AT{\vecV(\vgamma(\tau), t)}{\tau=t}
\eeq
What this means is that
for a particle trajectory,  the clocks 
on the 
particle and on
the observer agree
(assuming classical, non-relativistic, time).
When the 
velocity field
is time independent,
this
distinction between
a streamline
and a particle trajectory, disappears.

Suppose $f:M\rarrow\RR$
is a scalar function
on an $n$ dimensional manifold $M$.
A {\bf constant or level surface} 
is an $n-1$
dimensional manifold defined by

\beq
f(\vecx)=k
\eeq
for some constant $k\in\RR$.
The tangent
vectors
$\vecT$ of
the constant surface satisfy

\beq
\ul{df}(\vecT)=
\vecT\cdot \nabla f=0
\eeq
Thus, the vector 
field $\vecN$ perpendicular to
the constant surfaces everywhere satisfies

\beq
\vecN =\nabla f
\eeq 
Note that if
$\vecT$ is the tangent vector
field of $f(\cdot)$
and $\vgamma$
is an integral curve of $\vecT$,
then 

\beq
\frac{df(\vgamma(t))}{dt}=
\dot{\vgamma}\cdot
\nabla f=0
\eeq
Hence, an integral
curve of $\vecT$ is confined  to
a single constant 
surface of $f(\cdot)$ and

\beq
f(\vgamma(t))=k
\eeq

A {\bf flow}
$\vPhi^t(\pi)$
is a family of
integral curves,
just like a congruence,
but with more 
mathematical structure (trappings)
associated with it.
$\vPhi^t(\pi)$ is quantitatively
equal to a path
$\vgamma(t; \pi)$
that depends 
on time $t$
and a point
$\pi\in M$, but
$\pi$ is now the 
independent variable and 
$t$  is now the parameter. So
$\pi$ and $t$
have switched roles.

\beq
\vPhi^t: M\rarrow M, \quad
\vPhi^t(\pi) = \vgamma(t;\pi),
\quad \vPhi^0 (\pi)=\vgamma(0;\pi) =\pi
\eeq

A flow $\vPhi^t$
satisfies

\beq
\vPhi^s\circ\vPhi^t=
\vPhi^{t+s}
\eeq
whenever $\vPhi$ 
is defined at those time parameters.
Hence, the superscript $t$
in $\vPhi^t$
behaves like a power.

A flow moves a point in an integral
curve to another point
on the same
integral curve. 

One can always find a flow that
preserves the constant surfaces
of a scalar function $f(\cdot)$

\beq
f(\vPhi^t(\pi))=
f(\vgamma(t;\pi))=k= f(\vgamma(0;\pi))=f(\Phi^0(\pi))= f(\pi)
\eeq
so

\beq
f(\vPhi^t(\pi))= f(\pi)
\eeq
To find such a flow,
first find the vector field $\vecT$
that is tangential to the constant
surfaces of $f(\cdot)$, then
find the integral curves $\vgamma(t;\pi)=\vPhi^t(\pi)$
of that vector field.

A {\bf foliation} is a decomposition of a manifold into disjoint submanifolds called {\bf leaves}, which look  locally like parallel flat subspaces.
For example, a congruence
is a foliation of a manifold into 
1-dim leaves. Constant surfaces are a
foliation of an $n$ dim manifold into 
$n-1$ dim leaves.

The Frobenius theorem (see Section \ref{sec-Frob-theorem}) gives necessary and sufficient conditions for a {\bf distribution} to be locally tangent to a foliation.
