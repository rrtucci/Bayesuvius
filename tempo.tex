\section{Symplectic Manifold (Hamiltonian Mechanics)}


Symplectic manifolds
find their inspiration 
in Hamiltonian Mechanics (HM),
a sub-field of Classical Mechanics.

A {\bf symplectic manifold} $(M, \ul{\Sigma})$
is defined as a $2\nn$ dimensional manifold $M$ equipped with a 2-form
$\pform{\Sigma}{2}$
which satisfies 2 constraints

\begin{enumerate}
\item closed

\beq
d\ul{\Sigma}=0
\eeq

\item gives a non-degenerate
inner product

\beq
\ul{\Sigma} (\vecX, \vecY)\neq 0
\eeq
for all nonzero $\rvX, \rvY$.
\end{enumerate}

Compare this to a {\bf \Rien manifold} which is equipped with a metric $\metric(\vecX, \vecY)$

\subsection{Darboux Theorem}

\begin{claim}(Darboux Theorem)

Let $(M, \ul{\Sigma}$
be a $2\nn$ dimensional symplectic manifold. 
Then for every point $\pi\in M$,
there exists canonical phase space coordinates 
$(q_i, p_i)_{i=1}^\nn$
(called {\bf Darboux coordinates})
such that
if we define

\beq
\ul{\s}
\eqdef
\sum_i \ul{dq^i}
\wedge \ul{dp_i}
\eeq
then

\beq
\ul{\Sigma}=\ul{\s}
\eeq
\ul{in an open patch
containing $\pi$.}
\end{claim}
\proof
\qed


This means that in the future,
we can assume that
$\ul{\Sigma}=\ul{\s}$.

This also means that 
Symplectic theories are
unique once you fix the manifold dimension $\nn$.
The manifold doesn't have
any distinguishing features
like curvature.

Two simplest examples of symplectic theories are the symplectic plane $(\RR^2, \ul{\s})$ and the 
symplectic sphere $(S^2, \ul{\s})$.
For these 2 cases,
finding a possible
choice of Darboux coordinates is trivial. Darboux Theorem is much more amazing for $\nn>1$ because then
a lot of diagonalization occurs in order to achieve the canonical 2-form  $\ul{\s}$

\begin{enumerate}

\item \begin{claim} A possible choice of Darboux coordinates
for the symplectic plane $(\RR^2, \ul{\s})$
where

\beq
\ul{\s}= f(x, y)\ul{dx}\wedge \ul{dy}
\eeq
where $f(x,y)\neq 0$
is

\beq
q=x,\quad
p(x, y)= \int_{y_0}^y dy'\;
f(x, y')
\eeq
\end{claim}
\proof

\beq
dq = dx
\eeq

\beq
dp= f(x,y) dy
\eeq

\beq
\pder{(q,p)}{(x,y)}=
\left|
\begin{array}{cc}
1&0
\\
\partial_x p & \partial_y p
\end{array}
\right|=\partial_y p=f(x,y)\neq 0
\eeq
\qed


\item 
\begin{claim} A possible choice of Darboux coordinates
for the symplectic sphere $(S^2, \ul{\s})$
where

\beq
\ul{\s}=R^2 \sin\phi\;  \ul{d\phi}\wedge\ul{d\theta}
\eeq
is

\beq
q=\theta,\quad
p=R^2\cos\phi
\eeq
\end{claim}
\proof

\beq
dq = d\theta
\eeq

\beq
dp=-R^2\sin \phi d\phi
\eeq


\qed

Note that these coordinates are not global coordinates for the sphere.
The coordinate $\theta$ fails at the north and south poles, and doesn't return to
its initial value after one turn.

\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Classical Mechanics Review}
This Appendix presents 
some results from
Classical Mechanics 
that are alluded to in this section.
These results were 
discovered without the use of dif-forms
and can be understood 
without them. Dif-forms give a new 
interpretation to them and suggest generalizations of them.

Suppose we have break up the body into $N$ particles with $N$  {\bf positions} $\{\vecr_\alp\}_{\alp=1}^N$ and
$n$  {\bf generalized coordinates }
$\{q_i\}_{i=1}^n$.




\beq
\vecr_\alp=
\vecr_\alp(q^1, q^2, \ldots, q^n)
\eeq
for $\alp=1, 2, \ldots, N$.

$\vec{F}^\infty_\alp=$ {\bf non-contact force}
on particle $\alp$

$\vec{F}^0_\alp=$ {\bf contact force}
on particle $\alp$.
A contact force is a physical force that arises because two bodies are physically touching.
Non-contact forces 
are forces that act at a distance, such as gravity (in the Newtonian description).
Contact forces  are associated with {\bf motion constraints}. For example, if a bead is constrained to a wire, the wire exerts a
contact  force that keeps the bead on the wire. Constraints can be specified thus:


{\bf Newton's Law} for particle $\alp$ says

\beq
m_\alp \ddot{\vecr}_\alp = \vec{F}^\infty_\alp
+ \vec{F}^0_\alp
\eeq


\beq
\vec{F}^0_\alp\cdot
\delta \vecr_\alp=0
\eeq

$\delta \vecr_\alp=$
{\bf virtual displacement} of particle $\alp$.
A virtual displacement
is a man imagined displacement that is not necessarily the displacement that the particle actually makes during the next instant of time.
However, it must be consistent with the constraint.

$\delta W=
\sum_\alp \vec{F}_\alp
\cdot \delta 
\vecr_\alp=$ {\bf virtual work}

{\bf D'Alembert's principle}

\beq
\sum_\alp
(\vec{F}_\alp   -
m_\alp \ddot{r}_\alp)\cdot \delta \vecr_\alp=0
\eeq



$T=$ {\bf kinetic energy}

$V=$ {\bf potential energy}

$L(\vecq, \dot{\vecq}, t)=T-V=$ {\bf Lagrangian}

{\bf Euler-Lagrange's Equations}

\beq
\frac{d}{dt}\pder{L}{\dot{q}}^i -
\pder{L}{q^i}=0
\eeq
for $i=1, 2, \ldots, n$.

$H(\vecq, \vecp, t)=T+V=$ {\bf Hamiltonian}

{\bf Hamilton's equations}

\beq
\dot{q}^i= \pder{H}{p_i},\quad
\dot{p}_i =-\pder{H}{q^i}
\eeq
for $i=1, 2, \ldots, n$.


\begin{claim}

\beq
L_\alp=T_\alp-V_\alp,\quad H_\alp= T_\alp+V_\alp
\eeq
for a point particle $\alp$
for a point particle 
experiencing a conservative force
$-\nabla V_\alp$.
(For multiple particles,  $L=\sum_\alp L$, $H=\sum_\alp H_\alp$)

\end{claim}
\proof 

We'll drop the  particle
subscript $\alp$ in the 
confines of this proof.

The Lagrangian is defined by

\beq
L(\vecr, \vecv)=
T(\vecv)
- V(\vecr)
\eeq
where

\beq
\vecv=\dot{\vecr}
, \quad
T = \frac{1}{2}\vecv^2
\eeq

$L$ is defined this way so that the Euler-Lagrange equations reproduce
Newton's equation.
If $\vecr=(x^1, x^2, x^3)$ and $\vecv = (\dot{x}^1, \dot{x}^2, \dot{x}^3)=(v^1, v^2, v^3)$, then

\beq
\pder{L}{v^i}=
mv_i,
\quad
\pder{L}{x^i}= -\pder{V}{x^i}
\eeq

\beq
0=\frac{d}{dt}
\pder{L}{v^i}-
\pder{L}{x^i}=
m\ddot{x_i}+\pder{V}{x^i}
\eeq 
so

\beq m\ddot{\vecr}= -\nabla V
\eeq
The Hamiltonian
is defined by a Legrendre transform as
\beq
H(\vecr, \vecp)=
\sum _i p_i v^i - L(\vecr, \vecv)
\eeq
where 

\beq
p_i=
\pder{L}{v^i}=
m v^i
\eeq
Therefore

\beqa
H&=& \sum_i m(v^i)^2-L
\\
&=&
\frac{1}{2}m\vecv^2 
+
V 
\\&=&
T+V
\eeqa







\qed

historical path:

\eeq

\begin{claim}
The Euler-Lagrange equations also follow from 
extremizing 
the action.




\end{claim}
\proof

Consider the 
Lagrangian

\beq
L = L(\vecq,
\dot{\vecq}, t)
\eeq
We want to extremize
its action $S(t_2|t_1)$
using calculus of variations].

\beq
S(t_2|t_1)=
\int_{t_1}^{t_2}
dt\;
L(\vecq(t)
, \dot{\vecq}(t), t)
\eeq
We want the physical trajectory $\vecq(t)$ to make $S(t_2|t_1)$ stationary.
Take a one-parameter family of nearby curves

\beq
q^i(t, \eps)=
q^i(t)
+
\eps \eta^i(t)
\eeq
where the endpoints
are fixed:

\beq
\eta^i(t_1)=
\eta^i(t_2)=0
\eeq
Note that

\beq
\pder{{q}^i}{\eps}=
{\eta}^i,\quad
\pder{\dot{q}^i}{\eps}=
\dot{\eta}^i
\eeq
Assume the action is stationary

\beq
0=
\AT{\frac{dS}{d\eps}}{\eps=0}
=
\int_{t_1}^{t_2}dt\;
\AT{\frac{dL}{d\eps}}{\eps=0}=
\int_{t_1}^{t_2}dt\;
\left(
\pder{L}{q^i}
\eta^i
+
\pder{L}{\dot{q}^i}
\dot{\eta}^i
\right)
\eeq

\beq
\pder{L}{\dot{q}^i}
\dot{\eta}^i=
\frac{d}{dt}
\left(
\pder{L}{\dot{q}^i}
{\eta}^i
\right)
-{\eta}^i
\frac{d}{dt}
\pder{L}{\dot{q}^i}
\eeq
Integrate by parts

\beq
0=
\left[
\pder{L}{\dot{q}^i}
{\eta}^i
\right]_{t_1}^{t_2}
+
\int_{t_1}^{t_2}dt\;
\left(
\pder{L}{q^i}
-
\frac{d}{dt}
\pder{L}{\dot{q}^i}
\right)\eta^i
\eeq
Boundary terms vanish because $\eta^i=0$ at endpoints. The variational functions $\eta^i$
are arbitrary so integrand must be zero.


\qed

\subsection{Hamiltonian Theory}

Define {\bf configuration space}
as a manifold $M=\{\{q^i\}_{i=1}^\nn\in \RR^{\nn}\}$ parametrized by $\nn$ position $q^i$.
 
Define {\bf 
phase space}
as a manifold $M=\{(q^i, p_i)\}_{i=1}^\nn\in \RR^{2\nn}\}$ parametrized by $\nn$ position $q^i$, and $\nn$ momenta $p_i$. 

\beq
\pform{\lam}{} =\sum_i q_i \pform{dp_i}{}
\eeq

\beq
\pform{\s}{2}=\pform{d\lam}{2}=
\sum_i \pform{dq_i}{}
\wedge \pform{dp_i}{}
\eeq

\beq
\vecX=
\sum_i
\left[
q_i \vecpder{q^i}+
p_i \vecpder{p^i}
\right]
\eeq

\beq
\vecV=\dot{\vecX}=
\sum_i
\left[
\dot{q}_i \vecpder{q^i} +
\dot{p}_i \vecpder{p^i}
\right]
\label{eq-vecv-def-ham}
\eeq

\begin{claim}
Hamilton's equations given by
\beq
\dot{q}_i=\pder{H}{p_i},
\quad
\dot{p}_i=-\pder{H}{q_i}
\label{eq-ham-eqs}
\eeq
for  $i=1, 2\cdots, \nn$ are satisfied iff

\beq
\begin{array}{cc}
\bcen
\xymatrix{
\pform{dH}{}\ar[d]_1
& \pform{i_\vecV\s}{}\ar[dl]|1
\\
\Circle{0}&\pform{\s}{2}\ar[u]|{i_\vecV}
}\ecen
&
\pform{i_\vecV \s}{}
=
\pform{dH}{}
\end{array}
\eeq

\end{claim}
\proof

\beq
\xymatrix@R=3pc{
\pform{dq}{}
\ar[d]|{\partial_q H}
\ar@/_1pc/[dr]|<<<{\dot{p}}
&\pform{dp}{}
\ar[dl]|{\partial_p H}
\ar[r]|q
\ar[d]|{-\dot{q}}
&\pform{\lam}{}
\ar@/^2pc/[dd]|d
\\
\pform{dH}{}
\ar[d]^1
&\pform{i_\vecV\s}{}
\ar[dl]|1
\ar[d]_>>>>1
&\pform{i_\vecV\s}{}
\ar[dl]|1
\\
\Circle{0}
&\Circle{0}
&\pform{\s}{2}
\ar[u]|{i_\vecV}
}
\eeq


\beq
i_\vecV \pform{dq_i}{} =\dot{q}_i,
\quad
i_\vecV \pform{dp_i}{} =\dot{p}_i
\eeq


\beqa
i_\vecV\pform{\s}{2}
&=&
\sum_i
i_\vecV[\pform{dq_i}{}\wedge
\pform{dp_i}{}]
\\
&=&
\sum_i
\left[\ul{i_\vecV dq_i}\wedge \pform{dp_i}{}
-
\pform{dq_i}{}\wedge \ul{i_\vecV dp_i}
\right]
\\
&=&
\sum_i \left
[\dot{q}_i\pform{dp_i}{}
-
\dot{p}_i \pform{dq_i}{}
\right]
\eeqa

\beq
\pform{dH}{}=\sum_i
\left[
\pder{H}{q_i}\pform{dq_i}{}
+
\pder{H}{p_i}\pform{dp_i}{}
\right]
\eeq
Comparing the last two equations
for $i_\vecV\pform {\s}{2}$ and
$\pform{dH}{}$, and assuming they are
equals yields Hamilton's equations, given by
Eq.(\ref{eq-ham-eqs})

\qed

\subsection{Poisson Brackets}

For a scalar function $f(\;)$,
define the {\bf Hamiltonian vector
field $\hamfield{f}$} by

\beq
i_{\hamfield{f}}\ul{\Sigma} =
\ul{df}
\eeq

Note that 
if $\vecV$ is vector field defined
in Eq.(\ref{eq-vecv-def-ham}) then Hamilton's equations can be written as

\beq
\vecV= \hamfield{H}
\eeq

\begin{claim}
In the phase canonical
coordinates,



\beq
\hamfield{f}=
\sum_{i=1}^n
\left(
\pder{f}{p_i}
\vecpder{q^i}
-
\pder{f}{q^i}
\vecpder{p_i
}
\right)
\eeq

\end{claim}
\proof

Let 

\beq
\hamfield{f}=
\sum_i \left(
A^i \vecpder{q^i}
+ B_i \vecpder{p_i}
\right)
\eeq

\beq
\ul{\Sigma}=\ul{\s}=
\sum_i \ul{dq^i}
\wedge \ul{dp_i}
\eeq
Then

\beqa
i_{\hamfield{f}}
\ul{\s}
&=&
\ul{dq^i}(\hamfield{f})
\ul{dp_i}
-
\ul{dp_i}(\hamfield{f})\ul{dq^i}
\\
&=&
A^i\ul{dp_i}
- B_i \ul{dq^i}
\eeqa
Also

\beq
\ul{df}=
\sum_i\left(
\pder{f}{q^i}
\ul{dq^i}
+
\pder{f}{dp_i}
\ul{p_i}
\right)
\eeq
so

\beq
A^i = \pder{f}{q^i}
,\quad
B_i = -
\pder{f}{p_i}
\eeq

\qed

For two scalar functions $f(\;)$ and $g(\;)$,
define their {\bf Poisson bracket} by

\beq
\{f, g\}=
\sum_{i=1}^\nn
\left(
\pder{f}{q^i}
\pder{g}{p_i}
-
\pder{f}{p_i}
\pder{g}{q^i}
\right)
=
\sum_i
\underbrace{\det \pder{(f, g)}{(q^i, p_i)}}_{\text{det of Jacobian Matrix}}
\eeq

\begin{claim}

\beq
\{ f, g\}=
\ul{\s}\left(
\hamfield{f}, \hamfield{g}
\right)=
\oper{\hamfield{g}}(f)=-\oper{\hamfield{f}}(g)
\eeq
\end{claim}
\proof

\beq
\hamfield{f}=
\sum_{i=1}^\nn
\left(
\pder{f}{p_i}
\vecpder{q^i}
-
\pder{f}{q^i}
\vecpder{p_i
}
\right)
\eeq

\beq
\hamfield{g}=
\sum_{i=1}^\nn
\left(
\pder{g}{p_i}
\vecpder{q^i}
-
\pder{g}{q^i}
\vecpder{p_i
}
\right)
\eeq

\beqa
\ul{\s}(
\hamfield{f}, \hamfield{g})
&=&
\sum_i
\left(
\ul{dq^i}(\hamfield{f})
\ul{dp_i}(\hamfield{g})
-
\ul{dp_i}(\hamfield{f})
\ul{dq^i}(\hamfield{g})
\right)
\\
&=&
\sum_{i=1}^\nn
\left(
\pder{f}{q^i}
\pder{g}{p_i}
-
\pder{f}{p_i}
\pder{g}{q^i}
\right)
\eeqa

\beq
\oper{\hamfield{g}}(f)=
\sum_{i=1}^\nn
\left(
\pder{g}{p_i}
\vecpder{q^i}
-
\pder{g}{q^i}
\vecpder{p_i
}
\right)f
\eeq

\beq
\oper{\hamfield{f}}(g)=
\sum_{i=1}^\nn
\left(
\pder{f}{p_i}
\vecpder{q^i}
-
\pder{f}{q^i}
\vecpder{p_i
}
\right)g
\eeq
\qed

\begin{claim}
Hamilton's equations can be 
written as

\beq
\dot{f}=\{f, H\}
\eeq
for $f= q^i, p_i$.

\end{claim}
\proof

\beq
\dot{f} =
\sum_{i}
\left(
\pder{f}{q^i}\dot{q}^i
+
\pder{f}{p_i}\dot{p}_i
\right)
\eeq

\beq
\{f, H\}=
\sum_{i=1}^\nn
\left(
\pder{f}{q^i}
\pder{H}{p_i}
-
\pder{f}{p_i}
\pder{H}{q^i}
\right)
\eeq


\qed
\subsection{Canonical Transformations}

A {\bf canonical transformation} (CT) is
a change of phase space coordinates

\beq
\{(q^i, p_i)\}_{i=1}^\nn
\rarrow \{(Q^i, P_i)\}_{i=1}^\nn
\eeq
that \qt{preserves} the
form of the Hamiltonian
equations.
By this 
we mean that if
the old coordinates
satisfy

\beq
\dot{q}^i = 
\pder{H}{p_i}
,\quad
\dot{p}_i = -\pder{H}{q^i}
\eeq
for $i=1, 2, \ldots,\nn$, 
then the new coordinates 
satisfy

\beq
\dot{Q}^i = 
\pder{K}{P_i}
,\quad
\dot{P}_i = -\pder{K}{Q^i}
\eeq
for $i=1, 2, \ldots,\nn$.
Here $H(\vecq, \vecp)$ is the old Hamiltonian and
$K(\vec{Q}, \vec{P})$ is the new one.

A {generating function} for the 
CT satisfies 

\beq
\vecz = \begin{pmatrix}
\vec{p}
\\
\vec{q}
\end{pmatrix}
,\quad
\vecZ = \begin{pmatrix}
\vec{Q}
\\
\vec{P}
\end{pmatrix}
\eeq

\beq
A = \begin{pmatrix}
0& I_\nn
\\
I_\nn &0
\end{pmatrix}
\eeq

\beq
J_{Z/z}=\pder{\vecZ}{\vecz}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\pder{Z^1}{z^1}&\cdots&\pder{Z^1}{z^\nn}
\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots
\\
\pder{Z^\nn}{z^1}&\cdots&\pder{Z^\nn}{z^\nn}
\end{pmatrix}
\eeq
{\bf Vector Hamilton equations}

\beq
\dot{\vecz}=
A \nabla_\vecz H,\quad
\dot{\vecZ}=
A \nabla_\vecZ K
\eeq

\begin{claim}
The  following statements are equivalent
\begin{enumerate}
\item
Transformation $\vecz\rarrow \vecZ$ is canonical
(i.e., preserves the form of Hamilton's cequations)
\item
\beq
J_{Z/z} A J^T_{Z/z}=A
\eeq

\item
\beq \ul{dq}\wedge\ul{dp}= \pm \ul{dQ}\wedge \ul{dP}
\eeq
\end{enumerate}
\end{claim}
\proof
\qed
