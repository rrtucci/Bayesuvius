There is an important distinction between vectors at a point and vector fields:

A vector $\vec{v}\in T_\pi(M)$
lives at a single point $\pi\in M$.
A vector field $\vecX
\in \cup_{\pi\in M} T_\pi(M)$ assigns a vector $X(\pi)$
 to every point $\pi$.

A differential form $\ul{\omega}$ is also defined pointwise. When we write
$\ul{\omega}(\vecX, \vecY)$,
we really mean the function


\beq
\pi \mapsto
\ul{\omega}(\vecX(\pi), \vecY(\pi))
\eeq

The set $\cup_{\pi\in M}T_\pi(M)$
is called the {\bf fibre bundle}
of $M$.


\subsection{Differential forms, definition}

When discussing most
concepts about 
differential forms, I find it pedagogically 
advantageous to define the 
concept 
first for 0-forms, 1forms, 2-forms 
and then 
finally the general case of p-forms
(differential form with $p$ slots).
Henceforth, I will
follow that 
strategy.

Let 

$M=$ manifold (e.g. the surface of a sphere or Mobius strip)

$\pi=$ a 
point on $M$

$T_\pi (M)=$ 
tangent space at $\pi$.
A vector space.

$\ZZ_1^n = \{ 1,2,\ldots n\}$

$n=$ dimension of $T_\pi(M)$.

$\{\check{x}_i\mid i\in \ZZ_1^n\}=$ basis for $T_\pi(M)$. These vectors need not be orthogonal nor normalized.

We will always 
underline $p$-forms
and, except in special
cases, use
lower case 
Greek letter to represent them.
Sometimes,
we will be even 
more explicit and
write
$\pform{\omega}
{p}$ for a
$p$-form.
$\$^p$, where
represents $p$
place holders or empty slots.



\begin{itemize}
\item  0-form:

\beq
\ul{\omega} = f,\quad
f: M\rarrow \RR
,\quad
\pi \mapsto  f(\pi)
\eeq
For example, 
if
\beq
f(x,y)=x^2 + y \quad \text{and}
\quad \pi= (2,3)\eeq
then

\beq f(\pi)= 2^2 + 3= 7
\eeq

\item 1-form:

A 1-form is a LINEAR map

\beq
\ul{\omega}: T_\pi(M)\rarrow \RR,\quad 
\vecX\mapsto \ul{\omega}(\vecX)
\eeq
where $\vecX$ is an $n$-dim vector.

\beq
\ul{\omega}(\vecX)=\sum_{i=1}^n \omega_i \ul{dx^i}(\vecX)
\eeq

$\{\pform{dx^i}{}\mid i\in \ZZ_1^n \}$ 
are a basis for the 1-forms on $T_\pi(M)$. They satisfy

\beq
\ul{dx^i}(\check{x}_i)=\delta_i^j
\eeq
for $i, j\in\ZZ_1^n$.

For example, 
if $\ul{\omega}$ is the following 1-form \pformIN{=2}:
\beq
\pform{\omega}{}= 2 \pform{dx}{}
+ 5 \pform{dy}{},
\quad
\vecX = a \check{x}
+ b \check{y}
\eeq
then,
using $\ul{dx^i}(\check{x}_j)=\delta(i,j)$, 

\beq
\ul{\omega}(\vecX)=
2a + 5b
\eeq

\item 2-form:

A 2-form is a LINEAR map
\beq
\ul{\omega}: T_\pi(M)\times T_\pi(M)\rarrow \RR,\quad (\vecX,\vecY)\mapsto \ul{\omega}(\vecX, \vecY)
\eeq
where $\vecX$ and $\vecY$ are n-dim vectors.

\beq
\ul{\omega}(\vecX, \vecY)=\sum_{1\leq i < j\leq n} \omega_{ij} \ul{dx^i}(\vecX)
\wedge \ul{dx^j}(\vecY)
\eeq

$\{\pform{dx^i}{}
\wedge \pform{dx^j}{}\mid 
i\in \ZZ_1^n, j\in \ZZ_1^n,
i<j\}$ 
are a basis for the 2-forms on $T_\pi(M)\times
T_\pi(M)$. They satisfy

\beq
\ul{dx^i}(\vecX)
\wedge \ul{dx^j}(\vecY)=
\ul{dx^i}(\vecX)
\otimes \ul{dx^j}(\vecY)
-
\ul{dx^i}(\vecY)
\otimes \ul{dx^j}(\vecX)
\eeq
for $i, j\in\ZZ_1^n$.
Hence, 

\beq
\ul{dx^1}(\vecX)\wedge\ul{dx^2}(\vecY)=
-\ul{dx^1}(\vecY)\wedge\ul{dx^2}(\vecX)
\eeq
In other words, these basis elements 
 change sign 
if you exchange the
arguments $\vecX$ and $\vecY$.



For example, if
$\ul{\omega}$ is 
the following 2-form \pformIN{=2}

\beq
n=2,\quad
\ul{\omega}=
\ul{dx}\wedge \ul{dy},\quad
\vecX= X^1 \check{x}
+ X^2 \check{y}
,\quad
\vecY= Y^1 \check{x}
+ Y^2 \check{y}
\eeq
then
\beq
\ul{\omega}(\vecX,\vecY)= X^1 Y^2 -
X^2 Y^1= X^{UA(1}Y^{2)}
\eeq

\item $p$-forms

A p-form $\ul{\omega_\pi}$ is a LINEAR map

\beq
\begin{array}{l}
\ul{\omega_\pi}:
\underbrace {T_\pi(M)
\times
\cdots
T_\pi(M)}_{=T^p_\pi(M), 
\text{ $p$ copies of } T_\pi(M)}
\rarrow \RR,
\\
(\vecX^{(1)}, \vecX^{(2)}, \ldots, \vecX^{(m)})\mapsto
\ul{\omega_\pi}(\vecX^{(1)}, \vecX^{(2)}, \ldots, \vecX^{(m)})
\end{array}
\eeq
where $\vecX^{(j)}$ for $j=1, 2, \dots p$ are n-dim vectors.

Let 

\beq
\ul{\otimes  dx^{i_1 i_2\cdots i_p}}
(\vecX^{(1)}, \vecX^{(2)}, \ldots, \vecX^{(p)})
=
\ul{dx^{i_1}}(\vecX^{(1)})
\otimes 
\ul{dx^{i_2}}(\vecX^{(2)})
\otimes \cdots
\otimes 
\ul{dx^{i_p}}(\vecX^{(p)})
\eeq
where $\otimes $ is
Cartesian (order preserving) product for reals.

\beq
\ul{\wedge dx^{i_1 i_2\cdots i_p}}
(\vecX^{(1)}, \vecX^{(2)}, \ldots, \vecX^{(p)})
=
\ul{dx^{i_1}}(\vecX^{(1)})
\wedge
\ul{dx^{i_2}}(\vecX^{(2)})
\wedge\cdots
\wedge 
\ul{dx^{i_p}}(\vecX^{(p)})
\eeq



\beq
\cali^p =\{ I\mid I=(i_1, i_2, \cdots, i_p) , \quad 1\leq i_1, i_2,\cdots ,i_p\leq n\}
\eeq

\beq
\cali^p_< =\{ I\mid I=(i_1, i_2, \cdots, i_p) , \quad 0\leq i_1< i_2<\cdots <i_p\leq n\}
\eeq

\beq
\pform{\omega}{p}=
\sum_{I \in \cali^p_<} \omega_I
\pform{\wedge d x^I}{p}
=
\frac{1}{p!}
\sum_{I \in \cali^p}
\omega_I \pform{\wedge d x^I}{p}
\eeq


$\{\pform{\wedge dx^I}{}
\mid I\in 
\cali^p_<\}$ 
are a basis for the p-forms on $T^p_\pi(M)$. They satisfy

\beq
\ul{\wedge dx^I}(\vecX^{(I)})=
\sum_{\s\in S_p}sign(\s)
\ul{\otimes dx^I}(\vecX^{(\s(I))})
=
\ul{\otimes dx^I}(\vecX^{UA(I)})
\eeq
for $I \in \cali^p$,
where $\s$ ranges over all possible permutations of $p$ symbols.
Hence, 

\beq
\ul{\wedge dx^I}(\vecX^{(I)})=
-\ul{\wedge dx^I}(\vecX^{(\s (I))})
\eeq
if $\s$ is a swap (transposition)
of any two symbols of $I$.
In other words, these basis elements 
 change sign 
if you exchange any two 
arguments $\vecX$ and $\vecY$.


\end{itemize}

\subsection{Hodge star operator}

For every $p$-form $\pform{\omega}{p}$, the the $(n-p)$-form $
\pform{\star \omega}{n-p}$ is the unique form satisfying

\beq
\pform{\alpha}{p}
\wedge
\pform{\star\omega}{n-p}
= \av{\pform{\alpha}{p},\pform{\omega}{p}}\text{vol}
\eeq
for every $p$-form $\pform{\alpha}{p}$.

Here 
$\av{
\ul{\alpha},
\ul{\omega}}$ is the inner product on $p$-forms induced by the metric.


Suppose
$
\ul{\s^1},\ldots,
\ul{\s^n}
$
is an {\bf oriented orthonormal coframe} of 1-forms
Then
\beq
\text{vol}=
\ul{\s^1}\wedge\cdots\wedge \ul{\s^n}
\eeq

The Hodge star is especially simple on basis $p$-forms.
For example,
\beq
\star(\ul{\s^{i_1}}
\wedge\cdots\wedge 
\ul{\s^{i_p}}
)=
\pm 
\ul{\s^{j_1}}
\wedge\cdots\wedge 
\ul{\s^{j_{n-p}}}
\eeq
where the $j$'s are the complementary indices of the $i$'s, and the sign is chosen so that

\beq
(\ul{\s^{i_1}}
\wedge\cdots\wedge 
\ul{\s^{i_p}}
)
\wedge
\star(
\ul{\s^{i_1}}
\wedge\cdots\wedge 
\ul{\s^{i_p}}
)=
\text{vol}.
\eeq

Examples
\begin{enumerate}
\item $n=3,\RR^3$

Take the orthonormal basis
$
\ul{dx},\ul{dy},\ul{dz}
$
Then

\beq
\star 1
=
\ul{dx}\wedge \ul{dy}\wedge \ul{dz},
\eeq

\beq
\star \ul{dx} =
\ul{dy}\wedge \ul{dz},
\eeq
and cyclic permutations of $x,y,z$


\beq
\star(\ul{dx}\wedge \ul{dy})
=
\ul{dz}
\eeq
and cyclic permutations of $x,y,z$

\item 2-dim polar coordinates.

The coordinate coframe is
$
(\ul{dr}, \ul{d\theta})
$
but these are not orthonormal because
$
g
=
{dr}^2+r^2{d\theta}^2$.
Instead define the orthonormal coframe

\beq
\begin{array}{ll}
\ul{\s^1}=\ul{dr},& \check{x}^1 = \hat{r}
\\
\ul{\s^2}=r\ul{d\theta}, & \check{x}^2 =\hat{\theta}
\end{array}
\eeq
Now
$g= (1,1)$ so

\beq
\text{vol}=
\ul{\s^1}\wedge \ul{\s^2}=
r \ul{dr}\wedge \ul{d\theta}
\eeq

\beq
\star \ul{\s^1}=\ul{\s^2}, \quad
\star \ul{\s^2}=-\ul{\s^1}
\eeq
Hence

\beq
\star \ul{dr}=r\ul{d\theta},\quad
\star \ul{d\theta}=-\frac{1}{r}\ul{dr}
\eeq
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Contractions (Interior Product)}

A {\bf contraction, or interior product}
operator $i_\vecX$
for a vector field $\vecX$ maps a $p-$ form to 
a $p-1$ form.
It takes a $p$-form $\pform{\omega}{p}$
and puts $\vecX$ into
its first slot.


\begin{itemize}
\item 1-form:

\beq
i_\vecX(\pform{\omega}{})= \omega(\vecX)
\eeq

For example, suppose $\ul{\omega}$ is the following 1-form \pformIN{=2}:

\beq
\ul{\omega}=
a \ul{dx} + b \ul{dy} 
\eeq
and

\beq
\vecX= X^1 \check{x}
+ X^2 \check{y}
\eeq
Then

\beq
i_\vecX(\pform{\omega}{})= aX^1 + bX^2
\eeq


\item 2-form:

\beq
i_\vecX \pform{\omega}{2}=
\ul{\omega}(\vecX, \$)
\eeq
For example, if 
$\ul{\omega}$
is the following 2-form \pformIN{=2}

\beq
\ul{\omega} = \ul{dx}\wedge \ul{dy}
\eeq
and 
 \beq \vecX = X^1 \check{x} +
X^2 \check{y}
\eeq
then

\beqa \ul{i_\vecX\omega}(\vecY)&=&
\ul{dx\wedge dy}(\vecX, \vecY)
\\
&=&
\ul{dx}(\vecX)\otimes \ul{dy}(\vecY)
-
\ul{dy}(\vecX)\otimes \ul{dx}(\vecY)
\\
&=&
X^1 \ul{dy}(\vecY)
- X^2 \ul{dx}(\vecY)
\eeqa
Why the minus sign? To make 
he right side
anti-symmetrix
under swap
of $\vecX$ and $\vecY$.

\item $p$-form:

\beq
\pform{\omega}{p}=
\sum_{I \in \cali^p_<} \omega_I
\pform{\wedge d x^I}{p}
=
\sum_{I \in \cali^p_{<}}
\omega_I \pform{\otimes d x^{UA(I)}}{p}
\eeq

\begin{align}
\ul{i_{\vecX^{(1)}} \omega}(\vecX^{(2)}, \vecX^{(3)},\cdots, \vecX^{(p)})&=
\ul{\omega}
(\vecX^{(1)}, \vecX^{(2)}, \vecX^{(3)},\cdots, \vecX^{(p)})
\\
&=
\sum_{I \in \cali^p_{<}}
\omega_I \ul{\otimes d x^{UA(I)}}
(\vecX^{(1)}, \vecX^{(2)}, \vecX^{(3)},\cdots, \vecX^{(p)})
\end{align}

In general,
if $\ul{\alp}$
is a $p$-form
and  $\ul{\beta}$ is a
$q$-form, 

\beq
i_\vecX
(\ul{\alp}
\wedge \ul{\beta})
=
i_\vecX (\ul{\alp})
\wedge \ul{\beta}
+ (-1)^{deg(\alp)}
\ul{\alp}
\wedge
i_\vecX (\ul{\beta})
\eeq
where $deg(\alp)=p$.


\end{itemize}


\beq
\alp(\vec{\xi}, \$^p)_{j_1 j_2\cdots, j_p}=
\alp_{ij_1j_2\cdot j_p}\xi^i
\eeq

\subsection{Lie Derivatives}

The Lie derivative
measure how a quantity
changes along a trajectory.
It's defined for vector fields 
and $p$-forms

The {\bf Lie Derivative $\call_\vecV$  of a vector field
$\vec{U}$} equals

\beq
\boxed{\call_{\vecV}\vecU=
\left[
\vecV_{op}, \vecU_{op}
\right]\vec{r}}
\eeq

Coordinate expression

Suppose

\beq
\vecV_{op} = V^i\partial_i=
\vecV\cdot \nabla,
\quad
\vecU_{op} =  U^j \partial_j = \vecU\cdot \nabla
\eeq

\beqa
\call_\vecV(\vecU)
&=&
\vecV\cdot \nabla
[
\vecU\cdot \nabla \vec{r}
]
-
\vecU\cdot \nabla
[
\vecV\cdot \nabla \vec{r}
]
\\
&=&
[\vecV\cdot \nabla (\vecU)-
\vecU\cdot \nabla (\vecV )
]\cdot
\nabla \vec{r}
\\
&=&
[\vecV\cdot \nabla (U^i)-
\vecU\cdot \nabla (V^i )
]
\underbrace{\partial_i \vec{r}}_{\check{x}_i}
\\
&=&
\vecV_{op}(\vecU)-
\vecU_{op}(\vecV)
\eeqa


The {\bf Lie derivative of a $p$-form}
is given by
{\bf Cartan's Magic Formula}:

\beq
\boxed
{\call_\vecX = i_\vecX d + d\;i_\vecX}
\eeq
In Cartan's Magic Formula
\begin{itemize}
\item external derivative $d(\;)$ gives change at a point.

\item {Lie derivative} $\call_\vecX(\;)$ gives
change along a trajectory
\end{itemize}




$d(\;)$ raises the degree of a form by 1 and $i_\vecX(\;)$  
lowers it by one,
so both summands in Cartan Magic Formula
leave the degree
of a $p$-form
unchanged.

\begin{itemize}
\item 0-forms:

\beqa
\call_{\vecX}f()
&=&
i_\vecX(df) + \cancel{d(i_\vecX f)}^0
\\
&=&
\ul{df}(\vecX)
\\
&=&
\vecX\cdot \nabla f
=  \vecX_{op}(f)
\eeqa
directional derivative

\item 1-form:

Suppose $\omega$
is the following  1-form \pformIN{}

\beq
\ul{\omega}=
\sum_{i=1}^n
\omega_i(x)\ul{dx^i}
\eeq
and

\beq
\vecX =\sum_i x^i(x) \check{x}_i
\eeq

\beq
i_\vecX\ul{\omega}=
\ul{\omega}(\vecX)=
\omega_i X^i
\eeq

\beq
\boxed
{d(i_\vecX \ul{\omega})=
\partial_j
(\omega_i X^i)
\ul{dx^j}}
\label{eq-cmf-part1}
\eeq

\beqa
d\ul{\omega}
&=&
d \left[ \omega_i 
\ul {dx^i}\right]
\\
&=&
\partial_j{\omega_i}
\ul{dx^j}\wedge
\ul{dx^i}
\\
&=&
\frac{1}{2}
[\partial_j \omega_i -\partial_i\omega_j]
\ul{dx^j}\wedge
\ul{dx^i}
\eeqa

\beq
i_\vecX 
[\ul{dx^j}
\wedge \ul{dx^i}]=

\section{General Relativity}
Yes. In fact, differential forms provide one 
of the most elegant formulations of general relativity. 
{\bf Cartan} or {\bf tetrad (vierbein)} formulation
of General Relativity.



\hrule
 The metric

Ordinarily, one starts with the metric

\beq
\pform{g}{2}=g_{\mu\nu} \pform{dx^\mu}{}
\pform{dx^\nu}{}
\eeq

In the tetrad formalism, we instead introduce an orthonormal basis of one-forms,

\beq
\pform{e^a}{}=e\indices{^a_\mu}\;
\pform {dx^\mu} {}
\eeq

called the **coframe** (or tetrad).

The metric becomes

\beqa
\pform{g}{2}
&=&
\frac{g_{\mu\nu}}
{e\indices{^a_\mu}
e\indices{^b_\nu}}
\pform{e^a}{}
\pform{e^b}{}
\\
&=&\eta_{ab}\;
\pform{e^a}{}
\pform{e^b}{}
\eeqa

where

\beq
\eta_{ab}=\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,1,1)
\eeq

is the Minkowski metric.
Each Greek index $\mu, \nu, \ldots$ is replaced a Latin index
$a, b, \ldots$.

\hrule The connection 1-forms

Instead of the {\bf Christoffel symbols},

\beq
\Gamma^\rho_{\mu\nu},
\eeq

we introduce the 
{\bf connection 1-forms}

\beq
\pform{\omega\indices{^a_b}}{}=
\omega\indices{^a_{b\mu}}
\pform{dx^\mu}{}
\eeq

Metric compatibility implies

\beq
\omega_{ab}=-\omega_{ba}.
\eeq

{\bf Covariant derivative}
\beqa
DX^a &=& dX^a + \pform{\omega\indices{^a_b}}{}
\wedge X^b
\\
D &=& d + 
\pform{\omega}{}\wedge
\eeqa

 \hrule Cartan's first structure equation



The {\bf torsion 2-form} is defined by


\beqa
\pform{T^a}{2}
&=&
d\pform{e^a}{}
+
\pform{\omega\indices{^a_b}}{}
\wedge \pform{e^b}{}
\\
&=&
\pform{(De)^a}{2}
\eeqa

For General Relativity, we usually impose the torsion-free condition,

\beq
\pform{T^a}{2}=
\pform{(De)^a}{2}=0.
\eeq

\hrule Cartan's second structure equation

The {\bf curvature 2-form} is defined by

\beqa
\pform{
\Omega\indices{^a_b}}{2}
&=&
d\pform{\omega\indices{^a_b}}{}
+
\pform{\omega\indices{^a_c}}{}
\wedge
\pform{\omega\indices{^c_b}}{}
\\
&=&
\pform{(D\omega)\indices{^a_b}}{2}
\eeqa

This is the differential-form version of the Riemann tensor.
The curvature 2-form can be expanded as

\beq
\pform{\Omega\indices{^a_b}}{2}
=
\frac12
R\indices{^a_{bcd}}\;
\pform{e^c}{}
\wedge
\pform{e^d}{}
\eeq

Thus,

\beq
\pform{\Omega\indices{^a_b}}{2}
\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad
R\indices{^\rho_{\sigma\mu\nu}}
\eeq

\hrule Ricci tensor and scalar curvature

The Ricci tensor is obtained by contraction:

\beq
R_{ab}=R\indices{^c_{acb}}
\eeq

The scalar curvature is

\beq
R=\eta^{ab}R_{ab}.
\eeq

\hrule Einstein's equation

The Einstein tensor is

\beq
G_{ab}=R_{ab}-\frac12\eta_{ab}R.
\eeq



The energy-momentum tensor is replaced by the stress-energy three-form

\beq
\Sigma_a.
\eeq

Einstein's equations become

\beq
\pform{G_a}{3}
=
\frac12
\eps_{abcd}
\;
\pform{\Omega^{bc}}{2}
\wedge 
\pform{e^d}{}
=
8\pi G\;
\pform{\Sigma_a}{3}
\eeq

This is the differential-form version of

\beq
R_{\mu\nu}
-\frac12Rg_{\mu\nu}
=
8\pi G \Sigma_{\mu\nu}.
\eeq
\hrule Vacuum equations

For empty space,

\beq
\Sigma_a=0,
\eeq

so the field equations become

\beq
\boxed{
\eps_{abcd}
,
\Omega^{bc}
\wedge e^d=0.
}
\eeq

\hrule The whole theory in four lines



which is very much in the spirit of how Maxwell's equations become

\beq
dF=0,
\qquad
d(*F)=4\pi (*J).
\eeq

In fact, the Cartan formulation makes 
general relativity look strikingly similar to a gauge theory.

\xymatrix{
\pform{e^a}{}\pform{e^b}{}
\ar[d]|{\eta_{ab}}
\\
\pform{g}{2}
}
\eeq

\bcen
\xymatrix@C=3pc{

\\
