\section{Hopf Fibration}





Let

$
S^3=\{(x,y,z,t):\RR^4:
x^2+y^2+z^2 + t^2=1\}=$
surface of a sphere in $\RR^4$, or volume of a sphere in $\RR^3$

$S^3 = \{(\psip, \psim)\in \CC^2 :
|\psip|^2 + |\psim|^2 =1\}$= alternative way of describing $S^3$,
with $\psip=x+iy$, $\psim=z+it$

$
S^2 =\{(x,y,z)\in\RR^3 : x^2 + y^2 + z^2 =1
\}=$ 2-dim surface of a sphere in $\RR^3$

The {\bf Hopf  fibration} $h(\cdot)$ is the map
\footnote{A Hopf fibration (HF)
and a Hopf bifurcation (HB) are 
different constructions invented 
by the same person, Heinz Hopf.
They are connected in the sense that
in both subjects, circles arise naturally, but they play different roles. In HP, a circle is a fiber; in HB, a circle is a trajectory of a dynamical system.
The HF can arise naturally in the dynamics of a HB, especially when one studies oscillations with two complex amplitudes, as suggested by
the phasor picture of HF presented above.
}

\beq
{h}: S^3:\rarrow S^2,
\quad
{h}(\psip, \psim)
=\begin{pmatrix}
x\\y\\z
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
2Re(\psip\psim^*)
\\
2Im(\psip \psim^*)
\\
|\psip|^2 -|\psim|^2
\end{pmatrix}
\eeq


\begin{claim}
\beq
(x,y,z)\in S^2
\eeq
\end{claim}
\proof
\beqa
x^2 + y^2 +z^2 
&=&4|\psip\psim^*|^2
+ (|\psip|^2 -|\psim|^2)^2
\\
&=&
(|\psim|^2 + |\psip|^2)^2
\\
&=&
1
\eeqa
\qed

Let

$h^{-1}(x,y,z)=\{(\psipr,\psipi, z')\in S^3 : h(x,y,z)=(\psipr,\psipi,z')\}=$ {\bf fiber}, the inverse image of a point
$(x,y,z)\in S^2$  for a manifold (base space) $S^2$
For some $\xi\in\RR$,

\beq
h(e^{i\xi}\psip,e^{i\xi}\psim)
=h(\psip, \psim)
\eeq
So a curve parametrized by $\xi$
is the inverse image of $(x, y,z)$.
This {\bf global phase} $\xi$ cannot be measured by Quantum Mechanics, but it parametrizes the circular Hopf fibers.
In classical applications of the
Hopf fibration map, it can be measured
of course. 
\hrule
Visualization of Hopf fibers

In Section \ref{sec-stereo-proj},
we discussed stereographic projection (spros) from $S^2\subset \RR^3$ to $\RR^2$.
To visualize Hopf fibers, we will 
utilize spros from
$\RR^4$ to $\RR^3$ and from $\RR^3$ to $\RR^2$.

{\beq
\xymatrix{
(x,y,z)\in S^2\subset \RR^3
\ar[d]^{h^{-1}(x,y,z),\text{ inverse Hopf map}}
\\
{\begin{array}{c}
\left\{\vgamma(\xi)=(\psipr(\xi), \psipi(\xi), \psimr(\xi), \psimi(\xi))\in S^3: \forall \xi\right\}\subset \RR^4 
\\
\text{ closed curve in $\RR^4$}
\end{array}}
\ar[d]^{s_i(\;), \text{ first spro from $\RR^4$ to $\RR^3$}}
\\
{\begin{array}{c}
\{\vec{\Gamma}(\xi)=(X(\xi), Y(\xi), Z(\xi))\subset \RR^3:
\forall \xi \}
\\
\text{circle in $\RR^3$}
\end{array}}
\ar[d]^{s_2(\;), \text{ second spro from $\RR^3$ to $\RR^2$}}
\\
\text{closed curve in $\RR^2$}
}
\eeq}

Recall that the spro from $S^2\in\RR^3$ to $\RR^2$
maps injectively (in a 1-1 fashion)
the South Hemisphere (the surface) to a disk $D^1$ in $\RR^2$ and the North Hemisphere  (except for the
North pole) to  $\RR^2 -D^1$.
The South Hemisphere is compact (closed and bounded) and $D^1$ 
is compact to. This is what we expect since the spro is a continuous map. But note that
if we insist in mapping both hemispheres minus the North Pole,
the spro maps both
Hemispheres of $S^2$ minus the North Pole to a non-compact image,
all of $\RR^2$. Just that one point (the North Pole) makes the domain non-compact.

Similarly,
the spro from $S^3\subset\RR^4$ to $\RR^3$
maps injectively 
half of $S^3\subset \RR^4$ to the
an open ball in $\RR^3$,
but it maps all of $\subset \RR^4$ minus one point to all of $\RR^3$.


In the standard Hopf fibration, every pair of distinct fibers in $S^4$ is linked exactly once, and no two fibers intersect.
Stereographic projection of the fiber to 3-dim preserves this property 
as much as possible, but due to the limitations 
of 3-dim, it fails on some edge cases.

\hrule
Stereographic projection from $S^3\in\RR^4$ to $\RR^3$.

\beq
\psim =\underbrace{\psimr}_{\lam X} + i \underbrace{\psimi}_{\lam Y}
,\quad
\psip =\underbrace{\psipr}_{\lam Z} + i \underbrace{\psipi}_{\sqrt{1-\lam^2R^2}}
\quad \text{so }
\lam=\frac{1}{R} \sqrt{1-(\psipi)^2}
\eeq

\beq
|\psim|^2+|\psip|^2=1\rarrow
\quad
\lam^2\underbrace{[X^2 +Y^2 + Z^2]}_{R^2}+ 1-\lam^2R^2 =1
\eeq

\beq
\lam=1-\psimi
\eeq
so

\beq
R = \frac{1}{\lam}\sqrt{1-(\psimi)^2}=
\sqrt{\frac{1+\psimi}{1-\psimi}}
\eeq
and, inverting,

\beq
\psimi =\frac{R^2-1}{R^2+1},
\quad \lam=1 -\psimi = \frac{2}{R^2+1}
\eeq
\hrule
Parametrization of fiber


\beq
u^\pm = 
\sqrt{\frac{1\pm z}{2}}
\eeq

\beq
\psim = u^- e^{i\xi}
,\quad
\psip= u^+
e^{i(\xi+\theta)}
\eeq

\beq
x =2 Re(\psip \psim^*)= 2Re(u^+u^-e^{i\theta})=
\underbrace{2u^+u^-}_{\sqrt{1-z^2}}
\cos(\theta)
\eeq

\beq
y = 2Im(\psip \psim^*)= 2Im(u^+u^-e^{i\theta})=
\underbrace{2u^+u^-}_{\sqrt{1-z^2}}\sin(\theta)
\eeq

\beq
z = (u^+)^2 -(u^-)^2= \frac{1+z}{2}-\frac{1-z}{2}=z
\eeq


\beq
\left\{
\begin{array}{l}
\psipr
=u^+\cos(\xi+\theta)
\\
\psipi
=u^+\sin(\xi+\theta)
\\
\psimr
=u^-\cos(\xi)
\\
\psimi
=u^-\sin(\xi)
\end{array}
\right.
\eeq

\beq
\left\{
\begin{array}{l}
X(\xi)=\psipr/(1-\psimi)=
{u^+\cos(\xi+\theta)}/(1-u^-\sin(\xi))
\\
Y(\xi)=\psipi/(1-\psimi)=
{u^+\sin(\xi+\theta)}/(1-u^-\sin(\xi))
\\
Z(\xi)=\psimr/(1-\psimi)=
{u^-\cos(\xi)}/(1-u^-\sin(\xi))
\end{array}
\right.
\eeq

\beq
\lam = 1-u^-\sin \xi
\eeq
\hrule
Phasor picture

\beq
|\psip + \psim|^2 =
\underbrace{|\psip|^2 + |\psim|^2}_1 + \underbrace{\psip\psim^* +\psim\psip^* }_
{=2Re(\psip\psim^*)=x}
\eeq


\beq
|\psip + i\psim|^2 =
\underbrace{|\psip|^2 + |\psim|^2 }_1
+\underbrace{(-i\psip \psim^* +\psim\psip^*)}_{=
  2Im(\psip\psim^*)=y}
\eeq
(sign check: $\psim=1/\sqrt{2}$, $\psip=i/\sqrt{2}$ gives $|\psip+i\psim|^2=2$ and $1+y=2$ )

\hrule

\begin{claim}
The 
stereographic projection in $\RR^3$ of the fiber
$h^{-1}(x,y,z)$
is a curve $\vgamma(\xi)\in\RR^3$
which we parametrize with $\xi$.

\begin{itemize}
\item
$\vgamma(\xi)=(X(\xi), Y(\xi), Z(\xi))$ lies on the
 surface
of the sphere 

\beq
X^2 + Y^2 + Z^2 = R^2
\eeq
where

\beq R = \sqrt{\frac{1+z}{1-z}}
\eeq

\item
$\vgamma(\xi)$
lies in a plane 
whose normal vector is 
independent of $\xi$
and equals

\beq
\hat{N}=
\frac{1}{
\sqrt{2(1-z)}
}
(x, -y, -(1-z))
\eeq
\item
$\vgamma(\xi)\in\RR^3$
is the circle with center at

\begin{subequations}
\beq C_f=
\frac{1}{1-z}
(y,x, 0)
\eeq
and radius

\beq
r_f = \sqrt{\frac{2}{1-z}}
\eeq
\label{eq-hopt-center-radius}
\end{subequations}

\end{itemize}
 

\end{claim}
\proof

\beq
\psip = \frac{u^+}{u^-}
e^{i\theta}\psim
\eeq

\begin{subequations}
\beq
\psipr = 
\frac{u^+}{u^-}
[\cos\theta\psimr
-\sin\theta\psimi]
\eeq

\beq
\psimi = 
\frac{u^+}{u^-}
[\cos\theta\psimi
+\sin\theta\psimr]
\eeq
\end{subequations}







\begin{subequations}
\beq
Z = 
\frac{u^+}{u^-}
[\cos\theta X
-\sin\theta Y]
\eeq

\beq
\psimi = 
\frac{u^+}{u^-}
[\cos\theta Y
+\sin\theta X](1-\psimi)
\eeq
\end{subequations}



\beq
\frac{u^+}
{u^-}\cos\theta
= \frac{x}{1-z}
,\quad
\frac{u^+}
{u^-}\sin\theta
= \frac{y}{1-z}
\eeq
\hrule

\beq
xX-yY-(1-z)Z=0
\eeq

\beq
\hat{N}\cdot (X,Y,Z)=0
\eeq

\beq
x^2 + y^2 + (1-z)^2
=1 + 1-2z = 2(1-z)
\eeq


\beq
\hat{N}= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2(1-z)}}
(x, -y, -(1-z))
\eeq





\hrule
\beq
\frac{R^2-1}{R^2+1} = 
\left[\frac{x}{1-z} Y
+\frac{y}{1-z} X\right]\frac{2}{R^2+1}
\eeq

\beq
X^2 + Y^2 + Z^2-1-\frac{2x}{1-z}Y -\frac{2y}{1-z}X=0
\eeq

\beq
\left(
X-\frac{y}{(1-z)}
\right)^2
+
\left(
Y-\frac{x}{(1-z)}
\right)
=\underbrace{1 +
\frac{x^2+y^2}{(1-z)^2}}_{r_f^2}
\eeq

\beq
r^2_f=
1 +
\frac{x^2+y^2}{(1-z)^2}=
\frac{(1-z)^2+ 1-z^2}{(1-z)^2}
=\frac{2(1-z)}{(1-z)^2} =\frac{2}
{1-z}
\eeq
\qed

Eqs.(\ref{eq-hopt-center-radius}) 
for $C_f$ are interpreted
geometrically in Fig.\ref{fig-hopf-fiber-center}.
Turns out the center of Hopf fibers $C_f$ coincides with the point $P_-$ obtained
by stereographic projection of $(x,y,z)$.

From Eqs.(\ref{eq-hopt-center-radius})
for the center and radius of the fiber,
we see that if $(x,y,z)$ goes around 
the equator of $S^2$
or around any fixed $z$ (fixed latitude) circle on $S^2$, then $(X,Y,Z)$
generates a torus because the radius
of the fibers is fixed and their centers sweep out a circle.
Every fiber in the surface of the torus
links all the other fibers exactly 
once and no two fibers intersect. Lest you believe that is impossible,
see Fig.\ref{fig-hopf-keyrings}
that is a physical realization
of one of those tori.

As $z\rarrow 1$, the Hopf torus
degenerates to the line segment  $X=Y=0$, $Z\in[-1, 1]$.

We can generate a different 
torus by varying the latitude $z$.
All these tori have their greatest circle lying in the $Z=0$ plane.
