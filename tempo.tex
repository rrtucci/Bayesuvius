\section{The 3 Languages of Differential Geometry}



\begin{itemize}
\item The coordinate (holonomic)
basis is orthonormal
\beq
\vecpartial_\mu=\vecpder{x^\mu}, \quad
\av{\ul{dx^\nu}|\vecpartial_\mu}=\delta_\mu^\nu
,\quad
\vecpartial_\mu\cdot
\vecpartial_\nu= \delta^\mu_\nu
\eeq

\item Cartan's (no-holonomic) basis is in general neither
normalized nor orthogonal.
\beq
\av{\ul{\chi^b}|\vece_a}
= \delta^b_a,
\quad
\vece_a\cdot \vece_a \neq
1,\quad
\vece_a\cdot \vece_b \neq
0 \text{ for $a\neq b$}
\eeq
\item The t-basis used in curvilinear coordinates

\beq 
\vece_a=\frac{1}{\elli{a}} \vecpartial_{\phi^a},
\quad 
\ul{\chi^a}=
\elli{a} \ul{d\phi^a}
\eeq
is orthogonal but 
not normalized.
\end{itemize}


\beq
E\indices{^\mu_a}\ket{\vecpartial_\mu}=
\ket{\vece_a}
,\quad
E\indices{^a_\mu}\ket{\vece_a}=
\ket{\vecpartial_\mu}
,\quad
E\indices{^\mu_a}E\indices{^a_\nu}=\delta_\nu^\mu
\eeq

\beq
E\indices{^\mu_a}=
\av{\ul{dx^\mu}|\vece_a}
,\quad
E\indices{^a_\mu}=
\av{\ul{\chi^a}|\vecpartial_\mu},
\quad
\av{\ul{dx^\mu}|\vece_a}
\av{\ul{\chi^a}|\vecpartial_\nu} =\delta_\nu^\mu
\eeq


\beq
X\indices{^b_\nu}\bra{\ul{dx^\nu}}=
\bra{\ul{\chi^b}}
,\quad
X\indices{^\nu_b}\bra{\ul{\chi^b}}=
\bra{\ul{dx^\nu}}
,\quad
X\indices{^b_\nu}X\indices{^\nu_a}=\delta^b_a
\eeq

\beq
X\indices{^b_\nu}=
\av{\ul{\chi^b}|\vecpartial_\nu},
\quad
X\indices{^\nu_b}=
\av{\ul{dx^\nu}|\vece_b}
,\quad
\av{\ul{\chi^b}|\vecpartial_\nu}
\av{\ul{dx^\nu}|\vece_a}
=
\delta_a^b
\eeq

Note  the Dirac bra-ket notation
that we provide 
expresses the same
distinction that
we are already 
expressing by 
underlining 
dif-forms and
putting a vector
sign over vectors. 
Thus 
bra-ket notation
is redundant
in this case.
We are including
it here for pedagogical
purposes. It echoes
certain 
familiar properties such as
orthogonality
and completeness of a
basis. 

\beq
\text{Orthogonality: }
\av{\ul{dx^\nu}|\vecpartial_\mu}=\delta_\mu^\nu
\eeq

\beq
\text{Completeness: }
\av{\ul{\chi^b}|\vecpartial_\nu}
\av{\ul{dx^\nu}|\vece_a}
=
\delta_a^b
\eeq


Note that normally, if
an equation only has 
indices referring to a
single basis, we sometimes
use lower case Latin indices $i, a$, etc., whereas other times we use lower case Greek indices $\mu, \nu$, etc.
But in this section,
some equations refer to
2 different bases at the same time, and Latin indices refer to one basis,
and Greek indices refer to a different basis.

Note that we could replace $E\indices{^\mu_a}$
by its transpose $(E^T)
\indices{_a^\mu}$
and then its indices would be \qt{slopping upwards} instead of 
\qt{slopping downwards}.
Different authors choose
different conventions for this.
We have chosen the convention in which the
indices for both
$E\indices{^\mu_a}$
and its inverse $E\indices{^a_\mu}$
are both slopping downwards.
Likewise,
we have chosen the 
convention
in which the indices
of $X$ and its inverse are
both slopping downwards.
The important thing is that all of the equations above can be represented
in an intuitive,  unique and consistent way.
